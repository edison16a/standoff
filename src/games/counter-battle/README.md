# Paintball Battle

A team paintball game for up to four players on phones, one or two a side. It used to be called Counter Battle; the folder is still `counter-battle`. Your phone is the paint marker. Your fighter moves by themselves, circling the other team from bunker to bunker, while you aim, splat the other team and crouch when you want to go low. The first team to win five rounds takes the match.

Status: ready, on the home screen with its own icon, poster and clip.

## How to play

### Before the match

Pick Paintball Battle on the home screen and host a game. Everyone scans the code with their phone and types a name.

The big screen shows both teams, pink and cyan, with each player's name in their own colour, their gun and whether they are ready. The host picks 1 v 1 or 2 v 2 and the computer difficulty: Easy (the default), Medium, Hard or Training. In Training the computer players stand still and never shoot, for practice. New players land on the smaller team. Click a player to send them to the other side. With both teams full a player waits on the bench, and 2 v 2 lets them in. Computer players fill every empty place, so one player alone still gets a fight. Anyone not ready when the match starts sits it out.

Each phone takes three steps:

1. **Aim.** Hold the phone like a remote and point it at your own view on the big screen. A first page shows the grip. Your targets show inside your view, outlined in your colour. Point at the middle one and hold still until its ring fills. Each of the four corners follows by itself, with no button to press. Pointing past the edge of your view keeps your dot at the edge. Sweep round to check the dot follows you and press Looks good. A phone without motion sensors aims by dragging instead, so it only checks the drag here.
2. **Gun.** Pick one of the four and take it.
3. **Ready.** The match starts when the host says so.

### The guns

Every gun is a paint marker: a hopper or a paint magazine, an air tank for a stock and a ported barrel. They go thwup, not bang, and the balls fly slow enough to see.

| Gun | Shoots | Magazine | Best at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Paint Rifle | 20 a ball, about 8 a second, hold to fire | 30 | Long lanes. Steady and accurate. |
| Paint Shotgun | 9 balls of 13, tap to fire | 6, loaded shell by shell | Close in. It ends fights at a few paces. |
| Paint SMG | 14 a ball, 13 a second, hold to fire | 32 | Mid range. Its fighter runs the fastest. |
| Paint Sniper | 90 a ball, tap to fire | 5 | Far away. One ball to the mask ends it. |

Every fighter has 100 health. Hits to the mask count more. Damage drops off past each gun's range, most of all for the shotgun. Every gun kicks up and sideways as it fires, and a gun fired on the run spreads wider. Crouched, it is tighter.

### In the match

Your fighter moves by themselves, like the computer players do. They move up to a good distance from the other team for their gun, close for the shotgun and further off for the rifle and the sniper, and then keep circling round the other team at that distance. They slide from bunker to bunker, pause a moment at each so you get a steady shot, change direction now and then, keep away from their teammate and step out round the side of tall walls. When they are reloading or hurt they back off a little toward cover. All you do is aim, shoot, reload and crouch. Your view turns with your fighter to face the fight, and the crosshair stays right where your phone points while they move.

Tap **Crouch** to go low and tap it again to stand up. The button lights up and says Crouched while it is on. Your fighter stays low until you tap it again, on the move too, at about half the pace. Nothing else crouches you: not a low bunker and not a reload. Crouching never moves your aim: the view keeps its height and the crosshair stays on the same spot on screen as you go down and come back up. You see the duck as your fighter drops low in the picture. Press Shoot while crouched and your fighter rises for the shot, then drops back down a moment after the last one, with Crouch still on.

Point the phone at your view and press **Shoot**: hold it for the rifle and the SMG, tap it for the shotgun and the sniper. Your fighter slows down while you shoot, so the paint goes where you point. Behind a tall wall, a press steps your fighter out round the side first, and the shot goes once they are clear. Press **Reload** when the magazine runs low. The computer players can also kneel by themselves for a moment after a burst, or behind a low bunker to reload. A fighter on the move is harder for them to hit, and they only open fire once they are close enough. If the aim drifts, point at the middle of your view and press **Centre aim**. Phones without motion sensors aim by dragging round the Shoot button. The phone buzzes for hits, head shots, kills, taking a hit and going down.

Each player gets their own view over their fighter's shoulder. Two players share the screen side by side. Three or four take quarters, pink down the left and cyan down the right. Your name sits at the top of your view, your health at the bottom left and your magazine with a reload bar at the bottom right. The score, the round and a small map of the split sit at the top middle, and the kill feed at the top right.

A round ends when one side is all down. Round banners call Round 3 (or Match point), Fight, and who took it. Sides swap every round. First to five rounds wins. The results open on the winners' scene, and a moment later every fighter's kills, deaths, head shots and damage come up in the bottom right corner. Then Play again with the same teams, or Menu to go back to the lobby and change teams, guns or the match. The Standoff logo at the top left goes home to pick another game.

### The winners' scene

When the match is won the results open on the winning team together on a stage spattered with paint, under spotlights with confetti in their colour, as the camera swings slowly round. The top scorer lifts the gold cup overhead. Their names drop in big across the top in gold, each with their own colour under it, with the score under the names. Once the names have landed, the table and the Play again and Menu buttons come up in the bottom right corner, and the room's code comes back in the bottom left for anyone new to scan in.

## Technical notes

### The engine

`engine/` is pure TypeScript with no drawing and no timers. `Battle` steps at 60 steps a second from a seed, so the same seed and inputs always play out the same way. It holds the field and its cover graph, the fighters, their guns, the movement brain, the computer players' aim and the round and match clock. What happens comes out as `BattleEvent`s: shots with every ball's trace, hits, kills, reloads and the round and match results. The engine lands a ball at once; the picture and the sound follow it at `BALL_FLIGHT` speed.

Every fighter, player or computer, moves with the skirmish movement (`skirmish.ts`, `engage.ts`). A fighter hops along the cover graph a few metres at a time, so they never walk into a bunker or off the field. Each hop is picked from the spots a short run away (`pickHop`), scored on holding the gun's distance to the nearest enemy (`engage` in `tactics.ts`: 16 metres for the rifle, 21 for the sniper, 11 for the SMG and 7 for the shotgun, closing in as pressure builds over the round), on moving round the middle of the other team the way the fighter circles, on a line to shoot along, on cover, on room from teammates and on a hop of a good length. A hop that would cross a teammate's run, or go straight back, costs more. Teammates circle opposite ways, and each turns round every six to eleven seconds or when the field's edge stops them. At each spot the fighter pauses a quarter to most of a second, stepped out round the side if the spot is behind a tall wall. Starting a reload or dropping under 40 health makes them think again at the next spot, with four metres more distance and cover counting for more. While shooting a fighter moves at about half the jog. The body and camera turn to face one enemy and only switch to another when that one is clearly nearer, so the view never flicks between two.

A player's crouch (`Battle.setCrouch`) is a switch on the phone and a held state here: on, they stay low, still or on the move at 0.55 of the jog, until it is turned off, and nothing else crouches a player. Pressing Shoot (`Battle.setTrigger`) brings a crouched player up for the shot and they drop back down 0.6 seconds after the last one. A player's shot waits until they are clear of the cover: up to standing, or out round the side of a wall (`steppingOut` in `peek.ts`). A phone that drops hands the fighter to the computer until it is back. Computer players (`bot-aim.ts`) have a reaction time, an aim error that settles, a turn speed, a head shot chance and recoil control for each level, and after a burst or a single shot they often kneel for a moment. They never shoot at what they cannot see. In a skirmish their aim trails a running target (0.3 seconds of its run on Easy, 0.2 on Medium, 0.12 on Hard), so moving really helps, and they hold their fire past 1.6 times their gun's distance, so a round opens with both sides closing in rather than trading paint across the whole field. The levels come from the shared `games/kit/difficulty`: Easy, Medium and Hard map onto this game's own skills and run speeds, and Training leaves the computer players standing where they started, never shooting.

The cover brain (`brain.ts`, `peek.ts`, `plan.ts`) is the older movement: cover to cover, long hides and short peeks. The showcase's filmed fight was played with it, so `showcaseBattle` asks for it (`movement: "cover"`) and the trailer stays true until it is filmed again.

Tests: `skirmish.test.ts` plays a player against a still computer player and checks each gun closes in and holds its distance, circles both ways over a wide arc, and over long fights of computer players and of players never stands still for long, never ends up in a bunker or off the field and keeps teammates apart. `engage.test.ts` checks a hop is a short run that never lands on anyone, closes in from far off, backs off when reloading or hurt, follows the way the fighter circles and steers clear of a teammate. `crouch.test.ts` covers the crouch switch, the slower crouched move, rising for a shot and dropping back, no crouch on a reload, stepping out round a wall to shoot, and Training.

### The world

`render/` draws a `Battle` with three.js. It only reads the battle, so a replayed match looks the same.

* `arena/`: the turf with its lines and worn patches, the bunkers built to the engine's own shapes (so a ball stops where the eye sees the bunker), the nets on posts, the stands with fans who cheer on kills, the pit tents, the sky, hills and trees, and the late afternoon sun with shadows.
* `models/`: the four characters on one rig. The Paintball Pro wears a padded jersey and a mask with a mirrored lens. The Operator has a plate carrier, a helmet with a headset and a balaclava. The Street Runner has a hoodie, a bandana and a cap on backwards. The Heavy Gunner wears plates, shoulder guards, a bandolier and a visored helmet. Team colours tint the kit and the player's own colour marks each one. `models/guns/` has the four paint markers, built from the shared parts in `paint-kit.ts` (a hopper with the team's paint showing through its window, an air tank for a stock, a ported barrel), each with the points the hands hold and moving magazines, pumps and cocking handles.
* `anim/`: the animations, all worked out from the fighter's state each frame. The legs stand, run in any direction while the body faces the fight, kneel behind cover, and move crouched in a low walk with short flat steps (`lowWalkLegs` in `legs.ts`), reaching their feet by IK. The body turns side on to aim, leans out round tall cover, flinches from hits, falls when shot down and celebrates a round won in each character's own way. The hands work each gun: a magazine change for the rifle and SMG, shell by shell for the shotgun, bolt and magazine for the sniper, the pump after each shotgun blast and the bolt after each sniper shot. Tests check that the heads match the engine's hit boxes, that every helmet hides under the lowest bunker, and that both hands hold every gun.
* `effects/`: a puff of air at the barrel, the paintballs in flight (glossy, in the team colour, widened for each view so a far one still shows), a pop of paint where each lands, and a burst of paint when a fighter goes down. The splats are decals from an atlas drawn in code (`splat-art.ts`): sixteen shapes with lumpy edges, flung drops and streaks, and paint that is thicker and glossier in the middle. On walls the splat turns so its drips point down, and a shader lets them run down the wall over a couple of seconds (`splats.ts`, one instanced draw, capped at 360, oldest painted over first). A hit on a fighter projects a splat onto the body part it struck (`body-splats.ts`), so it rides every move; ten a fighter at most, a few built a frame. Everything is cleared each round.
* `victory/`: the winners' scene on the results, built in the shared victory kit's own room. The winning team stands together on a low stage, the top scorer on the left lifting a gold cup overhead in the free hand while the others celebrate each in their own way (`liftTrophy` in `anim/victory.ts`). Each winner wears splats in the losers' paint, projected onto the body parts like the hits in a match, and the floor and the stage are spattered in both colours (`team-paint.ts`). Spotlights, confetti in the team's colour and a slow circling camera finish it. The camera looks a little off centre so the team stands left of the middle, clear of the results panel, and stands back far enough that the lifted cup stays under the names and everyone's boots stay in the picture. `team-stage.ts` holds the layout and the framing, with tests that check both for every character.
* `hud/pane-hud.ts`: each player's thin crosshair in their colour, opening with the spread and following the kick, hit markers (bigger for a head shot, red for a kill), and a red vignette when hurt that greys the view once down.
* `camera/`: the over the shoulder camera, close behind the fighter, which follows the way the fighter faces, rises when they kneel so the player still sees over low cover, moves to the other shoulder when cover close by would fill the view, pulls in when a bunker is behind, narrows like a scope when a sniper looks out, shows the kick, and after a fall rises and swings back over the body. `aim-ray.ts` turns a point in a player's view into what they are pointing at, for `Battle.aimAt`. The television camera fills a spare quarter.
* `layout.ts`: the split screen, in the shape the kit's `SplitMap` takes.
* `battle-renderer.ts`: one scene and one WebGL renderer for every view, each a scissored viewport. Name tags show teammates always and enemies only while in sight, so a tag never gives away someone behind cover.

### Aiming in your own view

The host gives the aim kit a zone per seat (`HostAim.setZone`), the lobby's split screen as it stands, so the calibration targets show inside that player's view and the phone's point from -1 to 1 spans the view. Each frame the host turns that point into a ray through the player's shoulder camera (`aimTarget`) and hands what it hits to `Battle.aimAt`, so the crosshair lands where the phone points. It does this before the battle steps, and again once the cameras have followed the fighters for the frame (`CounterHost.aimPlayers`, called by the canvas between moving the cameras and drawing). Without the second pass the crosshair trailed a frame behind a moving fighter by up to a dozen pixels; with it the crosshair sits on the phone's point, which `move-aim.test.ts` checks over twenty seconds of skirmish. Crouching never moves the aim. The shoulder camera keeps its standing height through a crouch, the gun's angles and the crosshair are worked out from the standing eye (`aimEye`), and the shot itself heads from the real eye to the point the phone picked. Before, the camera and the eye both dropped, so the same phone point landed lower and the crosshair fell onto the bunker's lip. `crouch-aim.test.ts` checks the crosshair holds both its point in the world and its place on screen through a crouch and back up. If the split changes after someone calibrated, say a third player joins and halves become quarters, the kit maps their points into the new view.

### The host

`host/` runs a room. `CounterHost` is the session: the lobby (`lobby.ts`), the match (`match-driver.ts`, which steps the battle at its fixed rate and passes on the aim, trigger, crouch and reload), the demo fight behind the lobby, and the line to the phones (`publish.ts`, `phone-link.ts`). `lineup.ts` deals the characters so no two fighters share one, and gives each computer player a gun its side lacks, a call sign and a free seat colour. `moments.ts` turns events into buzzes, the kill feed, banners and the announcer's calls. A phone that drops has the computer shoot for it until it comes back; its fighter moves as ever.

### Sound

`audio/` is all synthesised and goes through the room's buses. The lobby plays "Kit Up", A minor at 76 a minute: a slow dark pad, a deep held bass, vibes on the tune and a rim on the third beat. The match plays "Standoff", F minor at 124: a sixteenth pulse bass drives under a dark pad and ticking hats, a synth lead stalks through the A half and climbs to long notes in the B half, and toms fill into each half. Each tune runs sixteen bars before it repeats. For a match point the theme moves up a semitone to 130 a minute and the lead turns to brass. There is a sting for each round and a fanfare for the winner, and the music ducks under kills and the announcer. Rendered offline, the tunes sit at an RMS of 0.04 to 0.05 with peaks under 0.45. Every marker has its own pneumatic thwup: a burst of air through the barrel, a soft thump and a hiss of gas, bigger for the shotgun and sharper for the sniper, with the shotgun's pump and the sniper's cocking handle after each shot. Reloads are timed to the animations. When a ball lands you hear it pop, a wet pap and a spatter, over the thwap of an inflatable, a knock on wood or a ping off a drum, timed to the ball's flight. The player who lands a hit hears a tick, a ding for the head and a crunch for a kill; the one hit hears a thud. A crowd and a breeze fill the field, and an announcer calls the rounds and the winner through the browser's speech. With one pair of speakers for everyone, each sound is as loud as it is to the nearest player and leans toward their view.

### The showcase and the home screen media

`showcase/` is the game playing itself, a seeded 2v2 of hard computer players (seed 7) using the cover brain it was filmed with, for the home screen's media. The media have no words: the name tags are off, `film-lights.ts` turns the sky's fill down and adds a hard rim light and a warm key that follow the camera, and the page grades the picture with more contrast and a vignette.

* The loop is a wordless trailer cut from round four (`trailer.ts`, cameras in `lenses.ts`, played by `film.ts`). Blaze sprints up the flank toward a low lens with paint cracking off him. Nova rises from behind his bunker and fires past the camera. Over Nova's shoulder, as the players see the game, he paints Kite's mask from the far end, and Kite goes down in slow motion. Then Blaze's own view as he charges Vex through a spray of paint, the shotgun blast that ends it in slow motion from low beside the bunker, and Blaze standing over the fallen Vex as the round is won. The fight only ever runs forward: a cut may skip ahead, and slow motion is half speed, one engine step to each filmed frame, so no frame is held. The capture blends the last second over the first, so the clip loops smoothly.
* The poster holds Blaze's shotgun going off into Vex four paces away, paint bursting on Vex's mask, from low on the near side with the big round bunker between them.
* The icon is staged key art rather than a frame of the fight (`key-art.ts`). The pink hero kneels with his back to the plywood wall in his own base, marker held across his body, facing a low lens. Past the end of the wall his team trades paint with the cyan side under a bright sky, and one of theirs goes down in a burst of pink. The Paintball Battle logo sits at the foot. `?fight=1` on the icon view shows the old frame from the fight instead, and `key-art.test.ts` checks the scene plays out the same way every time.

`trailer.test.ts` and `script.test.ts` fail if an engine or bot change moves these moments, as a reminder to film again. To film them, with the dev server running:

```bash
node tools/media/capture.mjs counter-battle --url http://localhost:3000 --ffmpeg /path/to/ffmpeg
```

### Testing in a browser

In development the host exposes `window.__cb` (the session) and `window.__cbRenderer`, and each phone `window.__cbPhone`. `__cb.turbo = 2` runs the match twice as fast and `__cb.roundsToWin = 2` plays a short match. `node tools/phone-fit.mjs --games counter-battle,counter-battle-touch` checks every phone page.

### Looking around in development

`/showcase/counter-battle` plays the loop, and `?review=1` holds it for a script, which moves it on with `window.__cbTrailer(seconds)`. `?view=poster` and `?view=icon` show the stills. `?panes=4` (or 2, or 1) splits the screen with a camera behind each fighter, `?seed=` plays another fight, `?at=seconds` holds a still at that moment, `?cam=x,y,z,tx,ty,tz` pins the television camera, and `?lite=1` draws without shadows at a lower resolution, which a software renderer on a busy machine can manage. `?lab=1` swaps the fight for the animation lab: the four characters in a row, each with a different gun, standing, running forward, sideways and back, kneeling, standing up to fire, reloading, taking two hits, falling and celebrating, on a 24 second loop. On a still, `window.__cbFilm(seconds)` steps on and draws, so a script can take a frame sequence at any rate. `?winners=2` (or 1) shows the results with sample players instead: the winners' scene, their names and the panel, for looking it over.
